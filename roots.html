Girmitiya roots

From a ship's register back to a village in UP

Between 1834 and 1916 well over a million Indians sailed as indentured labourers to colonies from the Caribbean to the Pacific. For Fiji, historians estimate about 80% of North Indian emigrants from the 1880s onwards came from what is now Uttar Pradesh.

What “girmit” means

Emigrants signed an “agreement” — pronounced girmit — binding them to a plantation for a fixed term, usually five years. Those who made the journey called one another jahaji bhai and jahaji behen, ship brother and ship sister. Mauritius received the first indentured Indians in 1834, British Guiana in 1838, Trinidad and Jamaica in 1845; Fiji's recruitment ran from 1879 until the system ended in 1916. Over 60,000 people went to Fiji alone.

Where in UP they came from

Early recruits came mostly from Bihar and the Chota Nagpur plateau. From the 1870s the eastern districts of the United Provinces took over as the main source. Historian Brij V. Lal names Basti, Gonda, Azamgarh, Sultanpur and Faizabad (now Ayodhya) among the districts that supplied the bulk of migrants, and his own family's story leads back to Bahraich. The records that unlock a village

Every emigrant was issued an emigration pass at the Calcutta (or Madras) depot. The pass usually recorded the person's name, father's name, age, caste, village, thana (police station) and zila (district), plus the ship and a depot number. Colonial clerks spelled Indian place names by ear, so “Fyzabad”, “Ghazeepore” or a village with three possible spellings is normal.

From record to village: the method

  1. Collect every family document and story; note any place name, however garbled.
  2. Get the emigration pass or immigrant register entry from the archive.
  3. Map the colonial spelling to today's district, tehsil and police station — boundaries and names have changed (Faizabad is now Ayodhya district, for example).
  4. Check the village against the khatauni and the parivar (family) register held locally.
  5. Visit with a local guide who speaks Awadhi or Bhojpuri, and go with modest expectations: sometimes the family is found, sometimes the land and temple are.
